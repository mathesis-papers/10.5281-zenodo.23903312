Resolving Pozsgay–Wanless's factor-perfection question and qubit decomposition conjecture

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

Can a network distribute information perfectly while its components fail to do so? For the three-component network here, Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang prove that classical perfection of the whole forces perfection of every component, extending an earlier linear result to arbitrary finite-alphabet maps. Their quantum argument also excludes representing any absolutely maximally entangled six-qubit state by this triangle with two-dimensional internal connections, even using arbitrary complex components.

The classical network starts with symbols x, y, z from one finite alphabet. A gate A transforms x,y into u,v; B transforms u,z into p,w; and C transforms v,w into q,r. The outputs are p,q,r, while u,v,w are internal. A gate is perfect when any two positions in its four-symbol input-output records uniquely determine the other two. The whole is perfect when any three exterior positions determine the rest. These records form orthogonal arrays: tables whose selected columns contain every possible tuple exactly once. Such patterns motivate constructions of exceptionally uniform quantum entanglement.

Pozsgay and Wanless had established the implication for linear gates and asked about nonlinear ones. The authors answer affirmatively without assuming invertibility. More strongly, they characterize perfect factors by six ways of arranging the exterior array as a matrix, called flattenings. Each divides the six symbols into row and column triples; the selected divisions keep one of the designated pairs xy, zp, qr together. Requiring these six matrices to be permutation matrices means unique matching between row and column labels. This is weaker than demanding all ten balanced divisions, equivalently all twenty three-column projections, be perfect.

The classical proof exploits counting without cancellation. Each selected exterior matrix factors into three matrices made by regrouping the gates' indices, carrying an untouched symbol along. Their entries are nonnegative integers counting compatible internal choices. The key lemma says that if a product of square nonnegative integer matrices is a permutation matrix, every factor must be one. Invertibility prevents zero rows, so integral row sums cannot fall below one; the product's row sums of one force equality, successively identifying permutation factors. The six divisions expose all local conditions needed for perfect gates.

Perfect gates nevertheless need not produce a perfect whole. On the three-symbol field, with arithmetic modulo three, take every gate to be F(a,b) = (a+b, a−b). Each gate is perfect: any two input-output entries recover the others. But the network's second output is q = 2x−z. Fixing x,z and changing y leaves q unchanged, so the exterior triple x,z,q cannot identify the input. This separates the six guaranteed divisions from full exterior perfection.

Quantum tensors replace binary indicators with complex weights and sum over internal connections. A perfect tensor is unitary, meaning reversible and length-preserving, under every balanced division of its indices. Its normalized state is absolutely maximally entangled: every half-system has a completely mixed reduced state. For ordinarily unitary gates, the authors prove the same six-division equivalence, supplying the reverse of Pozsgay and Wanless's construction. Regrouping preserves the sum of squared entry magnitudes. This fixed norm bounds each factor's determinant magnitude by one; a unitary product forces equality throughout, making every regrouped factor unitary.

Arbitrary complex components require replacement, not perfection of the originals: inverse changes at opposite ends of an internal connection preserve the whole but can spoil local perfection. The authors prove perfect replacements exist at the same internal dimension. Grouping exterior legs as xy, zp, qr expresses the triangle as three maximally entangled pairs acted on locally. Polar decomposition separates each local map into unitary and positive parts. Complete mixing of the grouped reduced states before and after the positive deformation makes a squared-norm function convex with equal endpoint derivatives. Consequently the positive parts leave the state unchanged; unitary replacements then satisfy the preceding argument.

At internal dimension two, replacement would require perfect four-qubit tensors, whose nonexistence is credited to Higuchi and Sudbery. This excludes the specified triangle decomposition, not six-qubit absolutely maximally entangled states themselves. The eight-qubit part of Pozsgay and Wanless's conjecture instead follows from known nonexistence of such states, credited to Rains and Scott. The manuscript reports Lean evidence only for the general classical factor-perfection implication; quantum results have written proofs, while finite exact checks of examples and small gate families are supplementary. Existence of nonlinear perfect classical composites at alphabet size four and of these quantum decompositions at local dimensions three and four remains undetermined here, as do unrestricted gate counts. Other network geometries are not settled.
